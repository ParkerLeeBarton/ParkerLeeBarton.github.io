Four seasons

The VEX Robotics Competition runs a new game every year: design, build and programme a robot to a fresh rule set with a fresh set of exploits nobody has found yet. I competed for four years on the Iron Eagles and was captain for all four, leading mechanical design, programming, CAD and the engineering notebook.

Over that period we won more than fifty awards and eight signature events, and made World Championship division finals twice. I also helped form what became the most successful alliance in VEX and built a network of top teams internationally to trade ideas and strategy with, which turned out to be worth more than any single mechanism.

Everything got documented, every season, and a lot of it got published to YouTube. A design that spreads through a competition is a design that was worth making.

Over Under: 9364C, 2023 to 2024

Hank

Our first robot of the season ran a strategy nobody else was running. Most robots that early depended on launchers to move balls from the matchloader. We put balls into the robot and pushed them across the field in bulk; we called it bowling. It made us first seed at Mall of America and then proved ineffective for that point in the season.

The strategy became dominant much later in the year, at which point we had a season of experience with it and everyone else was starting. Being early and wrong is often the same thing as being early.

Katie Ann

This one caught the field off guard. Teams were using blockers to stop shots from the matchloader, and rather than work around the blockers we raised the shooter on a lift and went over them. It gave us ball placement control nobody else had.

The robot won five tournaments including a signature event, and the design spread through the competition within weeks. It became one of the more influential builds of the season.

Charles

A refinement of Katie Ann. Better barrier crossing, a developed shooter and lift, and a modular system that let us swap between a slip launcher and a flywheel, trading speed for consistency depending on the match. It won two signature events.

Chris

For Worlds we kept the swappable system and added a third state: run neither mechanism. Stripped of both the flywheel and the launcher, the robot reverted to Hank’s bowling approach and fitted under six inches, which let us descore freely. One chassis, three strategies, chosen per match.

Robot in 3 Days

As part of the Why Not Alliance we hosted a Robot in 3 Days challenge and invited ten of the best teams in the world. Twenty-five people slept in my parents’ basement. We randomised the teams, built seven complete robots from nothing in three days, and ran a competition on the third to settle it.

The whole thing was filmed and published as a reveal and a documentary. Organising it taught me more about running a build than building did.

Spin Up: 9364C, 2022 to 2023

MK1

We won the first signature event of the season at Mall of America, which set up everything that followed. The robot was finished weeks before the tournament, which gave me an unusual amount of time for programming and strategy, and I used it to develop vision sensor tracking for goal position and roller colour.

The standout part was the blooper. It started as a scrap of metal and ended up on every robot in the competition once we showed it.

MK2 and MK3

MK2 kept the core and refined everything around it: more launcher height for range, and an intake roller that bobs under load and recovers cleanly instead of jamming. Unspectacular changes, much smoother robot. It won two signature events and made finals at two more.

MK3 is where I started laser-cutting custom Delrin in our lab, which opened up tight, complex mechanisms. The best of them was a ratchet that switched between a catapult and an intake, using custom-cut ratcheting gears and slip gears with teeth deliberately removed. It let us draw the catapult back faster to reload.

MK4 and MK5

MK4 introduced a large blocker that became common across the competition. A bound sheet of mesh sat folded inside the robot and deployed into a two-foot square screen, which could deflect endgame strings to limit scoring or push opponents’ strings out of the field entirely. It is largely credited with winning us the Canadian Open and reaching finals at the Kalahari Classic, where every opponent we faced in eliminations was disqualified because of it.

MK5 was the Worlds robot, built for reliability rather than novelty. The main addition was a third wheel mechanism that could turn rollers regardless of the robot’s approach direction. We ranked third in our division and reached the division final, where an electronics fault ended it.

Tipping Point: 9364E, 2021 to 2022

Ten bar

We were the first team to popularise a power takeoff in VEX. The mechanism shifts gears linearly to transfer power from one system to another, so the same motors drive the robot or run a mechanism depending on state. It worked well enough to make us the first Tennessee team to win a signature event.

Off the back of that season another team member and I formed the Why Not alliance, which became the most successful alliance in the programme’s history.

Tray robot

This was the first robot I built with CAD, and it changed how I work permanently. I started documenting everything, tracking what the strongest teams worldwide were building, and building the network of people I could think out loud with.

Change Up: 9364G, 2020 to 2021

My first season, on the freshman team, with seven people of whom very few did much. That pushed me into figuring out the whole thing myself, which is how I learned to build and how I taught myself to code.

The robots were bad. We built three that year, each one an attempt to be slightly less bad than the last, and we won one small tournament. It was the foundation for everything after it, and I would not remove it from this page: the gap between these and the Worlds robots three years later is the actual point.

What four seasons actually taught me

  • Read the rules like a specification. Half of our best mechanisms came from a rule change nobody else had costed out. The zip system, the configuration swap and the blocker all existed because somebody read the manual carefully.
  • Design for the strategy, not the mechanism. Chris was three strategies on one chassis. That is a systems decision, not a mechanical one.
  • Ship early, then iterate in public. Being finished weeks early gave me time for programming and strategy, which is where the tournament was actually won.
  • Documentation is leverage. Everything I know about notebooking, design reviews and change logs came from here, and it is the reason I can hand somebody a written case for a decision on a commercial project now.
